R2c — reranker comparison, in-domain fine-tuning, device parity, and the end-to-end value gate

2026-06-13 · config-v0.2.0 · branch feat/r2-retriever-upgrade-20260613 · successor to the Phase-0 spike and the P0–P4 plan

1. Offline, a fine-tuned tiny cross-encoder wins. Across every deployable reranker on the held-out test split, fine-tuning the 23 M MiniLM-L6 on the 159 k in-domain pairs (149 s of training) lifts rerank P@3 from 0.627 → 0.756 — past zero-shot mxbai-base (0.714, 8× larger) and within 0.04 of the multi-billion-parameter Qwen3 references (0.79). Its score also becomes far more thresholdable (chunk AUC 0.731 → 0.783, near the 0.80 R1 bar). The deployed int8 .tflite + on-device tokenizer reproduce the offline model exactly. Final deployable choice: fine-tuned MiniLM-L6.

2. End-to-end, that win does not carry. With the actual on-device generator (Gemma 4 E4B), reranking does not improve kenya SAQ answers, and a relevance diagnostic shows why — reranking doesn't improve kenya retrieval either (gecko is the best arm; P@3 0.277 vs 0.19–0.26). It's a distribution-transfer failure, not a model ceiling: the mamaretrieval gain (0.51→0.76) doesn't generalize to kenya, and low absolute relevance everywhere points to a corpus-coverage bottleneck. Do not ship the reranker for kenya; validate any retriever/embedder change on the deployment queries, not mamaretrieval. (An earlier +12% claim used the wrong generator, gemma3n-e4b, and is retracted.)

3. The dominant lever is the generator, not retrieval. A controlled 9-arm A/B shows Gemma 3n more than doubles kenya recall over Gemma 4 (0.115 → 0.270) and is far more complete on healthbench (+8 pp), uniformly across every retrieval arm — a much larger effect than any reranker/retriever change measured here (see Generator comparison below).

Read across any row and the thesis is unmistakable: offline P@3 ranges 0.41 → 0.82 (a 2× spread driven by reranking), but every deployment column is essentially flat — kenya answer recall ~0.11–0.13 regardless of config, healthbench overall ~0. The gains are real and large where they're measured (the audit set) and evaporate where they're used. Specifics:

  • The best offline config is the worst place to look for end-to-end proof. Hybrid+mxbai-ft tops offline (0.821) yet its kenya recall (0.113) is below plain Gecko (0.125).
  • mxbai-ft is the only reranker with any end-to-end pulse — a consistent but tiny completeness bump on the corpus-covered healthbench (Gecko 0.173 → 0.182, Hybrid 0.174 → 0.184; +~1 pp) with flat-to-lower penalty. The deployable MiniLM-ft shows none. Even mxbai-ft's gain is within noise.
  • BM25 is a kenya liability, end-to-end too. Its standalone kenya relevance is 0.06–0.08 (vs Gecko 0.27); end-to-end its kenya recall is 0.10–0.12 (≤ Gecko's 0.125) and healthbench overall ≈ 0 — confirming the lexical pool hurts, which is why Hybrid (which folds in BM25) sits below Gecko on the deployment queries despite winning offline.
  • healthbench overall ≈ 0 because Gemma 4 E4B triggers a penalty (harmful/incorrect) criterion ~37% of the time while meeting only ~17–18% of positive criteria — a generator-quality ceiling the +/− split exposes, orthogonal to retrieval.

Reading the table:

  • Zero-shot, size barely buys quality. Among off-the-shelf models the deployed MiniLM-L6 (23 M, 0.627) ties the much larger bge/ELECTRA bases (~0.64); only mxbai-base jumps to 0.714. MiniLM-L12 is no better than L6.

P2.5 — in-domain fine-tuning (the decisive lever)

We fine-tuned the deployed MiniLM-L6 on all 158,993 graded (query, chunk) pairs for the train-split queries (regression on grade/6, 2 epochs, DEV-selected, held-out TEST evaluated — no test query ever seen), then re-scored the same test pool. Training took 149 s on one H200.

Two findings:

  • Fine-tuning is the lever, not size. The fine-tuned 23 M MiniLM-L6 beats zero-shot mxbai-base (0.714) at one-eighth the size and, on the strict cut, exceeds the Qwen3-8B reference (0.380 vs 0.369).
  • R1's threshold is revived. Fine-tuned mxbai-base crosses the 0.80 chunk-AUC bar (0.859 ≥3, 0.857 ≥5) — and tops even the Qwen3-8B reference (0.792). For the first time a reranker's score is thresholdable by R1's own criterion, so the abstention/threshold idea R1 shelved for cosine is worth re-testing on a fine-tuned reranker score.
Deployment choice = fine-tuned MiniLM-L6. It is the best quality-per-byte (24 MB int8, 13 ms/pair, proven on the device) and already beats every zero-shot model. Fine-tuned mxbai-base is the quality-ceiling upgrade (≈8× the size/latency, ~2 s for a 20-chunk bundle) for if/when the threshold-revival or the extra ~0.065 P@3 justifies the footprint — it is not the default. The end-to-end value gate below uses the deployable fine-tuned MiniLM-L6.

P0 — does the deployed reranker reproduce the validated quality?

The in-app reranker is the int8 .tflite driven by a hand-ported Kotlin WordPiece tokenizer. We ran the actual deployed artifact (reranker_minilm_l6_int8.tflite) locally over all 9,820 test pairs, fed by both the HuggingFace tokenizer and a faithful Python port of the Kotlin tokenizer, at the deployed seq-len 256.

Note: this verifies the zero-shot MiniLM-L6 that is currently on-device; deploying the fine-tuned weights requires re-running the same (proven, architecture-identical) conversion + this parity check on the new checkpoint.

P1 — the end-to-end value gate (does reranking improve answers?)

Everything above is retrieval-quality evidence. The R2 standing rule is to validate end-to-end. Three arms isolate the effect, generated with Gemma 4 E4B (the actual on-device generator — app_config.json llm_model = gemma-4-E4B-it.litertlm) over the same questions:

  • A. Gecko top-3 — the currently deployed retriever (full-delta reference).
  • B. hybrid top-3 — Gecko+BM25 RRF (R2a); isolates fusion (A→B).
  • C. hybrid top-20 → fine-tuned MiniLM-L6 rerank → top-3 — isolates reranking (B→C).

Judged by the pinned gpt-oss-120b on key-fact recall / refusal / safety (SAQ: kenya + afrimedqa_saq), plus MCQ accuracy (afrimedqa, auto-scored — does the −1.8 pp RAG gap close?).

Result (Gemma 4 E4B — the actual on-device generator) — the offline retrieval win does not translate end-to-end. Two fine-tuned rerankers are tested as arm C against the same gecko/hybrid baselines (key-fact recall for SAQ, accuracy for MCQ; n in header):

Reading the corrected result honestly:

  • On the reliable sample (kenya SAQ, n=312), reranking does NOT improve key-fact recall. Gecko — the deployed retriever — is the best arm (0.125); hybrid and both rerankers are slightly lower (~0.111–0.113). The differences are within noise, so the honest read is "no benefit," not "regression."
  • afrimedqa_saq (n=37) hints at benefit (mxbai-ft 0.208 > minilm-ft 0.171 > gecko 0.157) but the sample is far too small to weight.
  • MCQ: the gain is mostly fusion. hybrid (0.524) beats gecko (0.481, +4.3 pp from fusion); reranking adds little on top — MiniLM-L6-ft 0.521 (≈hybrid), mxbai-base-ft 0.536 (the best MCQ arm, but only +1.2 pp over hybrid).
  • One real positive: the MiniLM-L6-ft rerank arm has the lowest harm rate (0.151 vs ~0.19); refusal stays near zero throughout.
Why the offline win didn't carry over. The strong offline reranking gain (P@3 0.51→0.76) was measured on the mamaretrieval query set; this value gate runs on kenya/afrimedqa — a different distribution. So either reranking improves retrieval less on kenya queries specifically, or Gemma 4 E4B already extracts what it needs from gecko's top-3 (a model-ceiling effect). The distinguishing diagnostic — measuring the reranker's P@3 on kenya directly, and how often the relevant chunk is already in gecko's top-3 — is the recommended next step.
Verdict: the reranker is a large, real win on offline retrieval metrics, but its end-to-end answer-quality benefit is not demonstrated on the reliable kenya SAQ sample with the actual device generator (Gemma 4 E4B) — and the relevance diagnostic below shows the cause is that reranking doesn't improve kenya retrieval either (a distribution-transfer failure). The current evidence does not justify shipping the reranker for kenya. (An earlier draft reported a +12% recall lift; it used the wrong generator, gemma3n-e4b, and is retracted.)

Diagnostic — why didn't reranking help kenya? (no-transfer, not a model ceiling)

The value gate left one question: is the reranked kenya context actually more relevant (so the bottleneck is Gemma 4 not using it — a model ceiling), or just different and not better (the reranker doesn't transfer to kenya)? kenya retrievals had no relevance labels, so we judged each arm's top-3 directly with the same rubric and judge (Qwen3-32B) that produced the 230k grades — 2,691 (query, chunk) pairs.

The answer is unambiguous: on kenya, reranking does NOT improve retrieval relevance — gecko (the deployed retriever) is the best arm. So this is no-transfer, not a model ceiling: the reranked context genuinely isn't more relevant, which is exactly why the answers didn't improve. The reranker's strong mamaretrieval gain (P@3 0.51→0.76) simply does not generalize to the kenya query distribution. Three things fall out:

  • It's a distribution-transfer failure, not Gemma 4 ignoring good context. Retrieval is still a legitimate lever in principle — but anything tuned on mamaretrieval must be re-validated on the real deployment queries (kenya / afrimedqa SAQ), not just mamaretrieval. This generalizes the R1/R2 guardrail.
  • Hybrid fusion (BM25) actively hurts kenya retrieval (0.277 → 0.204) — the R2a fusion adds lexical noise for these queries. The rerankers only reorder that degraded pool, so even the better one (mxbai-ft, 0.264) can't beat plain gecko. On kenya, the deployed gecko top-3 is the best of every option here.
  • The deeper signal is corpus coverage. Absolute kenya relevance is low across all arms (best mean grade 1.49 / 6) vs afrimedqa_saq (2.3–3.2). When even oracle-of-these-retrievers tops out this low, the bottleneck is likely the corpus not containing the answer for many kenya vignettes — a content gap a better retriever/reranker cannot fix. afrimedqa_saq, where reranking does help (mxbai 0.649 > gecko 0.477), confirms the method works when the corpus covers the query.

Gemma 3n beats Gemma 4 on every deployment metric — and the generator, not the retriever, is the lever.

  • kenya recall: 0.115 → 0.270 (≈2.3×), uniformly across all 9 arms (incl. BM25). The retriever/reranker barely moves either generator (both flat across the column); swapping the generator more than doubles answer completeness.
  • healthbench: overall −0.002 → 0.082, completeness 0.177 → 0.257 (+8 pp) — 3n covers far more rubric criteria.
  • And here it's not a safety trade: healthbench penalty is slightly lower for 3n (0.363 vs 0.373) — on this rubric 3n is more complete and at least as safe.
Safety nuance (honest): the healthbench rubric penalty favours 3n, but the earlier kenya-SAQ harm-rate metric (a different construct) had 3n higher (≈0.32 vs 0.19). So "is 3n safer?" is metric-dependent: on the structured healthbench rubric, yes; on the SAQ harm flag, no. What's unambiguous is that 3n is substantially more complete on both sets, and that the device's switch from 3n → 4 traded away a large amount of answer completeness. This is the highest-leverage finding for whoever owns the generator choice — far larger than any retriever/reranker effect measured here.
