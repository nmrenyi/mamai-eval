Phase A judge calibration — three-way result

MAM-AI v0.2 · HealthBench rubric · 6,853 physician-labeled triples · 2026-06-08

Headline. Five judges tested across the open-source bake-off (Maverick, Nemotron, gpt-oss-120b) and the closed-source escalation (gpt-5-mini, gpt-5). No judge lands at the physician calibration baseline. They sit on a clean spectrum of net judge met-rate (the direct driver of the headline `weighted_met` score): the largest open-weight candidates over-rate above the physician 77% baseline, the smaller gpt-oss-120b sits just below it, and the closed-source candidates progressively under-rate further — gpt-5 by ~19 pp. Within each family, capability pushes the calibration further from physicians in that family's direction.

Decision. No clear winner emerges. All three active candidates (gpt-oss-120b, gpt-5-mini, gpt-5) produce conservative headlines; the choice is between mildest understatement (gpt-oss-120b at −3.8 pp, but weakest per-criterion accuracy on NOT-MET rows) and stricter alternatives. Pending: gpt-5.4-mini result (~99% complete) for completeness. gpt-5.4-mini pending

Method

Each candidate judge runs the OpenAI HealthBench rubric grader over the same 6,853-row OBGYN-scoped meta-evaluation set (mamabench@v0.2.1/calibration/obgyn_meta_eval.jsonl), each row a physician-labeled (prompt, completion, criterion) triple. Closed-source judges use the OpenAI Responses API + Batch endpoint (reasoning.effort=medium, reasoning.summary=auto); open-source judge runs on a 1× H200 vLLM serve at the same effort level. We report the four standard meta-eval metrics plus the deployment-relevant judge met-rate calibration.

What the metrics measure

Each triple is a (prompt, completion, criterion). Physicians labeled each as MET (the response satisfies the criterion) or NOT MET. The LLM judge produces the same binary label. The metrics below summarize, in different cuts, how often the judge's label matches the physicians'.

About the labels: 163 distinct physicians contributed across the dataset, but each row has only 2–4 labels (93.5% of rows have exactly 2; 6.3% have 3; 0.2% have 4). "Consensus" below means a clear majority — for the dominant 2-label rows, that requires both physicians to agree (5,241 of 6,853 rows = 76.5% reach consensus this way; the rest are 1–1 ties, excluded from consensus metrics).

LLM ↔ single human
Across every physician-LLM pairing (each row may be labeled by multiple physicians, so this counts each pair separately), what fraction agree? The reference is the human ↔ human baseline (77.6%): physicians don't even agree with each other 100% of the time, so 77.6% is the ceiling an "average physician" judge could hit.
LLM ↔ physician consensus
Restrict to the rows where physicians reached a clear consensus (majority vote on MET or NOT MET, ignoring ties). What fraction of those does the judge get right? This is the cleanest single number — it removes noise from individual-physician disagreement.
Agreement on MET rows / NOT-MET rows
The consensus rows broken out by label. MET agreement = "of the rows physicians agreed are MET, what fraction did the judge also call MET" (high = good recall on quality). NOT-MET agreement = the same on the other side (high = the judge isn't rubber-stamping; it correctly flags poor responses). This split is the rubber-stamp detector — lenient-biased judges typically score near-100% on MET while scoring much lower on NOT-MET.
Judge met-rate (vs physician 77%)
Across all rows, what fraction did the judge label MET? Physicians' rate is 77.0% — distance from that is a direct measure of calibration bias. Below 77% = judge under-rates ("too strict"; false negatives); above 77% = judge over-rates ("rubber-stamping"; false positives). The deployment headline metric weighted_met inherits this bias directly.

Why two "agreement" metrics? Overall agreement can be high even when the judge is badly biased — e.g., 77% of rows are MET, so a judge that always says MET scores 77%. Splitting by MET/NOT-MET reveals the bias the overall number hides.

The capability-vs-bias tradeoff

Note on the two bias measures: a judge's per-class error asymmetry (when it's wrong, which way does it err?) and its net effect on the headline metric (judge met-rate vs physician met-rate) can move in different directions. gpt-oss-120b illustrates this: it makes more false-positive errors than false-negative ones on individual ambiguous cases (per-class lenient pattern), but its overall met-rate (73.2%) still lands below physicians' (77.0%) because it also misses 16% of physician-met rows. Interpretation

Across five real judges, the calibration forms a coherent spectrum, not a noisy scatter. Reading from lenient to strict: Llama-4-Maverick (88.8%) → Nemotron-Ultra-253B (83.3%) → gpt-oss-120b (73.2%) → gpt-5-mini (70.0%) → gpt-5 (58.3%). Two patterns stand out:

(1) Among the open-weight candidates, bigger models over-rate more, not less. Maverick (17B active params, MoE) and Nemotron (253B) are both more lenient than the smaller gpt-oss-120b. Capability did not buy calibration on the open side; it pushed in the wrong direction.

(2) Within the OpenAI lineup, the pattern inverts: bigger models under-rate more. gpt-5 is twice as strict as gpt-5-mini. The closed-source escalation argued in the budget doc — "open-weight over-rates, so we need closed-source to fix it" — turns out to be half true: closed-source does eliminate over-rating, but replaces it with progressively stronger under-rating.

The "gate metric" we initially singled out — agreement on NOT-MET rows — does improve cleanly with capability (gpt-5 is +14 pp over gpt-oss-120b, +37 pp over Maverick). But this is a side effect: a judge that rejects more rows mechanically agrees more with rejections. The cost is paid on the MET side. No judge across the seven data points (5 real + 2 trivial) achieves both calibration and per-class accuracy. The right question is no longer "which judge wins" but "which bias direction is acceptable for the v0.2 deployment story."

Decision implications

  • Single-metric escalation rule is invalidated. The budget doc's rule ("if not-met materially beats 67.9%, run Phase B at gpt-5-mini; else escalate") presupposed that improving NOT-MET-row agreement meant a better judge. It doesn't — it just means a stricter one, which moves the net headline further from physician calibration in the conservative direction.
  • No tier-jump fixes the calibration. Moving from gpt-5-mini to gpt-5 burns ~$32 of additional Phase A spend to confirm the pattern, not to fix it. Going higher (gpt-5.4, gpt-5.5) will most likely push further in the strict direction.
  • All three active candidates produce conservative headlines. No candidate in the active decision space would inflate `weighted_met` — every one of gpt-oss-120b, gpt-5-mini, and gpt-5 lands below the physician 77% met-rate. The choice is over how conservative:
    • gpt-oss-120b (−3.8 pp): mildest understatement; closest to physician calibration of any judge tested. Free to run (cluster GPU). The trade-off is the rubber-stamp pattern on individual cases — its NOT-MET-row agreement (67.9%) is the weakest of the three, so a meaningful fraction of borderline failures get marked MET. The headline is the most accurate; the per-criterion accuracy is the weakest.
    • gpt-5-mini (−7.0 pp): moderate understatement. Defensible "lower-bound" framing. ~$22 for Phase B.
    • gpt-5 (−18.7 pp): heavy understatement. Best per-criterion accuracy on NOT-MET cases (81.9%), but the deployment headline reads ~19 pp below true physician-equivalent quality, which is a hard story to tell. ~$135 for Phase B.
    • Bracket with two judges: use gpt-oss-120b (most-accurate headline) and gpt-5 (most-rigorous per-criterion verdict) as a pair, report weighted_met as an interval. The widest, most-honest range — though the lower bound (gpt-5's number) may be hard to defend as a useful figure. ~$135 incremental Phase B.

How to choose the final judge

The active decision space is three candidates: gpt-oss-120b (open, cluster), gpt-5-mini (OpenAI Batch), gpt-5 (OpenAI Batch). Four factors carry weight, each capturing a different aspect of "is this judge fit to trust." None of the three wins on all four — picking a judge means choosing which axis matters most.

Recommendation

By the data, gpt-oss-120b is the empirical winner — it wins 4 of 5 factors including the headline-calibration metric and costs nothing in API spend. The original "open-weight rubber-stamps, must escalate" argument from the budget doc is partially walked back by our own data: gpt-oss-120b's net headline shift (−3.8 pp) is the smallest of any judge tested, and Maverick / Nemotron's rubber-stamping does not generalize to gpt-oss-120b.

The one real concern is gpt-oss-120b's NOT-MET-row agreement (67.9%, below the human-human 77.6% baseline). For the v0.2 report, this is the disclosure to make explicit: "the judge is well-calibrated on the headline and on physician-met cases, but agrees with physicians on borderline failure cases only ~68% of the time, vs ~78% for physician pairs." If reviewers ask whether the judge under-detects failures, the answer is "yes, by about 10 pp below the human ceiling, and here is the per-category breakdown."

Footnote — reasoning-effort sensitivity test. A natural follow-up question is whether the NOT-MET weakness can be closed by giving gpt-oss-120b more compute to think with. We tested this directly by re-running the calibration at reasoning_effort=high:

High effort improves NOT-MET-row agreement by only +1.7 pp — well within bootstrap noise on a 774-row subset — while degrading every other metric by 4–5 pp. The NOT-MET weakness is not "reasoning-bound": additional compute does not fix it. This is consistent with the broader spectrum finding that within any judge family, more capability / more reasoning moves the judge in the strict direction (better NOT-MET, worse everything else), never toward physician calibration. Medium remains the right pin.

If failure-detection rigor is more important than the rest (a defensible position for clinical evaluation, but not the only one), Strategy C (bracket with two judges) is the methodologically strongest move — report the gpt-oss-120b number as the primary `weighted_met`, and the gpt-5 number as a conservative lower-bound disclosed alongside. The ~$135 incremental cost is well within the approved budget and the dual-judge methodology is the most-defensible-to-reviewers configuration on offer.

Selecting gpt-5-mini alone is the weakest option: it wins nothing, costs more than gpt-oss-120b, and shifts the headline further from physician calibration. Pick it only if there's an external reason to commit to a closed-source-API judge that isn't gpt-5.
