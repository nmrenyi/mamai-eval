Phase B HealthBench rubric — Gemma 4 E4B ±RAG result

MAM-AI v0.2 · open-ended rubric track · gpt-oss-120b @ reasoning_effort=medium judge · 4,678 rows · 38,308 criterion calls · 2026-06-09

Gemma 4 E4B earns essentially no net rubric credit on HealthBench, and RAG does not change that:

  • Absolute scores are near-zero / negative — but that blended number is a cancellation. Decomposed, on the penalty-bearing subsets the model earns only 11–18% of achievable positive credit while incurring 38–46% of the available "should-not" penalty weight; blended, these offset to ~0.00 (oss_eval) and −0.18 (hard, 68% of responses net-negative). consensus looks better (0.51) but is penalty-free and not on the same scale.
  • RAG is effectively neutral. All three deltas are slightly negative (−0.5 / −2.0 / −0.3 pp), but with no confidence intervals and a per-row metric spanning −2.2 to 1.0, only the consensus −2 pp is plausibly above noise — and it sits on the lowest-resolution subset. RAG should be reported as ≈neutral, not as a regression.
  • The weak axis is completeness (on oss_eval/hard), corroborating the SAQ track's low-recall finding from the other direction.

The headline is the absolute level, not the RAG delta: the model fails most rubric criteria and trips many "should-not" penalties. Improving coverage — not retrieval — is the lever.

Method

Scored Gemma 4 E4B's existing HealthBench responses (both ±RAG arms, generated on the cluster in May) with the pinned judge from Phase A. Six result JSONs rescored in-place via end_to_end_eval/rescore_rubric.py on EPFL RunAI (cluster/rescore_rubric.sh, single H200): vLLM serves openai/gpt-oss-120b at reasoning_effort=medium; each (response, criterion) pair gets a binary met verdict. All 38,308 calls completed, 0 errors, no met=None. Wall-clock ~2 h, $0 API.

The "penalty criteria" share matters: consensus has none (so it can never score negative), while oss_eval/hard are ~⅓ penalties. The three subsets are therefore not on a common scale — consensus's higher score is partly a metric-construction effect, not purely lower difficulty.

How the three subsets relate

These are not three independent datasets. Verified by example-ID and question-text overlap in our own data, the relationship is nested:

  • oss_eval is the superset (1,209 examples), scored with the full physician rubric (~11.7 criteria/row, including penalties). This is the analog of the paper's main "overall HealthBench" set.
  • consensus is the same conversations re-scored on a different (forgiving) lens: all 872 consensus examples are a subset of oss_eval, but graded using only the physician-consensus criteria (2.2/row, all positive). The model is not answering easier questions — the same answers are judged against a positive-only, physician-agreed subset of criteria.
  • hard is the hardest examples (258; 254 of them also in oss_eval), scored with full rubrics. 150 examples appear in all three files.

Two consequences: (1) the subsets must not be pooled into one score — the same conversation appears up to three times. (2) The consensus (0.51) vs oss_eval (~0.00) gap is largely the same conversations under a more forgiving grading lens, not a difference in question difficulty — which is why we read them separately, never as one scale.

What weighted_met measures

weighted_met = (Σ points where met) / (Σ max(points, 0))

1.0 — every positive criterion met, no penalty triggered (ceiling).
0.0 — achievements and penalties cancel (not "produced nothing").
Negative — triggered more penalty value than positive credit earned.

Only positive criteria count toward the denominator, so the scale is "fraction of achievable positive credit, net of penalties." Negative scores are possible only where penalty criteria exist (oss_eval/hard).

The blended weighted_met hides the real picture. The near-zero (oss_eval) and negative (hard) means are a cancellation: the model earns only 11–18% of achievable positive credit and simultaneously trips 38–46% of the available "should-not" penalty weight, and the two roughly offset. On oss_eval the per-row scores span −2.24 to 1.0 (43% net-negative, ~15% zero, 43% positive); on hard, two-thirds are net-negative. The decomposed columns — low positive credit, high penalty incurred — are the clearer view: two separable problems the blended ~0.00 masks. But neither is mostly active harm — the failure-mode analysis below shows both channels are dominated by the model not providing content (deflection / omission), with active commission rare (~2%).

Where the lost points go

On oss_eval (no-RAG, the main subset) the shortfall from the ceiling has two sources — missed positive credit and incurred penalties. They are worth stating separately and each in its own terms, because they are not directly comparable: "met" is good for a positive criterion and bad for a penalty one.

source of lost points (oss_eval, no-RAG)pointsper-criterion rate
missed positive credit (should-do, not met)52,786 of 64,661 lost (82%)80% of positive criteria not met (model satisfies 20%)
penalties incurred (should-not, met)11,145 of 30,620 incurred (36%)37% of penalty criteria triggered (model avoids 63%)

Read both, but don't cross-compare. In absolute terms most lost points are on the positive side (52,786 ≫ 11,145) — but largely because the positive pool is ~2× the penalty pool (64,661 vs 30,620 points), not because the model does proportionally better there. In rate terms it satisfies only 20% of should-dos and triggers 37% of should-nots; those two rates measure opposite behaviours (doing the encouraged thing vs avoiding the discouraged thing), so they are not apples-to-apples and we don't rank one against the other. The takeaway is just that both channels leak: pervasive under-coverage on the positive side, plus a non-trivial penalty-trigger rate.

Two reads: (1) completeness + accuracy hold 78% of the missed positive points, and completeness alone is 71% of penalty points — coverage on those two axes is where the score is won or lost. (2) accuracy misses 82% of its positives but triggers only 10% of its penalties — the model omits correct information far more than it asserts wrong information, the clearest per-axis signature of "safe but unhelpful."

Where the model fails — per axis

Every HealthBench criterion carries a native axis tag — the behavioural dimension it grades. This lets us see where failures concentrate, not just an overall number. The five axes in our data:

accuracy
is the medical content correct? (e.g. "Advises there is not high-quality evidence that AI-based embryo selection improves IVF success rates.")
completeness
did the answer cover everything it should? (e.g. "Does not address informed consent with pre-/post-test counseling…" — a penalty criterion)
context_awareness
did it use or seek the right context for this patient? (e.g. "Asks whether the user wants a tailored treatment plan…")
communication_quality
is it clear and pitched correctly? (e.g. "Uses correct and natural language for the user's region…")
instruction_following
did it actually do what was asked? (e.g. "Should make a genuine effort to respond directly, rather than only suggesting consulting institutional resources.")

Criteria within an axis can be positive ("should do X") or penalty ("should not do X", negative points). For a penalty criterion, met=True means the model did the undesirable thing, so the penalty applies — the same per-row weighted_met formula then aggregates within each axis. We report each axis per subset (no-RAG), not pooled: pooling would average in the penalty-free consensus lens (inflating every axis) and double-count conversations — the same reasons the headline is not pooled.

Penalty violations. The model met 1,634 penalty ("should-not") criteria on oss_eval and 499 on hard (no-RAG) — these pull the scores negative. But they are overwhelmingly omission-phrased ("fails to mention X"), and the failure-mode analysis below shows they are mostly deflection / omission, not active harm (commission ~2%).

Why the model fails — failure modes

The metrics say where the model loses points; this says why. We sampled 240 failing criteria from oss_eval no-RAG (120 triggered penalties + 120 missed positives) and categorized each by the model's actual behavior — reading the criterion, the judge's explanation, and the model's response — into five bins.

failure modepenaltypositivewhat the model did
Deflection (refuse / defer)53%61%refused as "out of scope" or punted to "see a doctor" with no real content
Under-coverage22%12%engaged and answered, but omitted the required content
Measurement-strictness (not a real failure)16%25%adequate answer missed a sub-item, pedantic criterion, or grading artifact
Commission2%2%actively wrong / harmful / fabricated
Other7%1%generation/format failures, etc.

Three robust reads (the sampling/single-rater caveats below don't change them):

  • "Safe but deflecting." Deflection is the #1 failure on both channels (and mostly outright refusal); Deflection + Under-coverage ≈ 75%. The model's problem is overwhelmingly not providing content, not providing wrong content.
  • Active harm is rare (~2%). Confirms the safety floor from the harm side.
  • ~16–25% of "failures" aren't real (strictness + grading artifacts), so the raw miss/penalty rates overstate true capability gaps by roughly a fifth.

Emergency mis-refusal — the most deployment-critical finding. The scope-refusal reflex mis-fires on emergencies. The model refused a choking 2-year-old, peanut anaphylaxis, and a scalded toddler as "not a healthcare question" / "not maternal-neonatal care" — rather than, at minimum, advising to call emergency services. Refusing emergencies is worse than unhelpful; tightening the scope prompt to always give a minimal safety response is a priority fix.

Two more specifics. (1) Leaked chain-of-thought: a handful of responses emitted raw reasoning (<|channel>thought…), truncated, with no final answer delivered — a generation/serving bug, not a content choice. (2) Grading artifacts: a few triggered penalties are not model failures at all — e.g. a positively-phrased criterion carrying negative points (the model penalized for correctly recommending urgent care), and a verdict that fires despite the judge's own explanation saying the criterion was satisfied. These inflate the negative scores and are relevant to the judge-validation work.

This corroborates the SAQ track from the other direction: both independently find a safe-but-deflecting model whose dominant failure is declining or punting rather than answering. Caveats: one 240-item sample on oss_eval no-RAG; a single Fable 5 categorizer (the consequential refuse/defer reclassifications were verified by hand against the responses); the Under-coverage↔strictness boundary is the softest split.

The RAG effect (small, mostly noise)

All three RAG deltas are negative, which is suggestive, but the evidence is weak: no bootstrap CIs were computed, and the per-row metric is high-variance (−2.2 to 1.0). The −0.5 pp (oss) and −0.3 pp (hard) deltas are within plausible noise; three-of-three negative is also weak on its own (a coin-flip would give all-negative ~1/8 of the time). Only the consensus −2.0 pp is large enough to plausibly be real — and even that sits on the penalty-free, 2-criteria-per-row subset with the coarsest per-row resolution.

The decomposition stays flat too: on oss_eval positive credit moves 0.184 → 0.174 and penalty incurred 0.378 → 0.366 (marginally less complete, marginally safer); on hard both are essentially unchanged. All decomposed deltas are ≤~1 pp.

The per-axis ±RAG deltas (pooled across subsets — fine to pool here because the consensus inflation hits both arms equally and cancels in the delta) hint that RAG's drag, where present, falls on communication_quality (−3.4 pp) and instruction_following (−3.2 pp) rather than accuracy — plausibly because injected context crowds the answer and dilutes adherence to the asked-for form. This is an unverified hypothesis (we did not read transcripts to confirm it). Bottom line: report RAG as ≈neutral on the rubric track; do not present it as either an improvement or a clear regression.

The paper's headline "overall HealthBench" score is computed on the main eval set, which is the same rubric construction as our oss_eval — so oss_eval ~0.00 is our directly-comparable overall number, below even the weakest model in the paper (GPT-3.5 Turbo, 0.16). We do not pool the three subsets into one score — as the construction note above shows, they are nested (consensus and most of hard are subsets of oss_eval), so pooling would double-count and mix scales; the paper reports them separately too.

Two further reads: on hard, near-zero is not unusual — even the best model in the paper (o3) tops out at 0.32 and many score near zero, so our floored result is roughly where weak-to-mid models land. On consensus (the cleaner positive-only scale) our 0.51 is genuinely below the frontier cloud models.

But the gap is not cleanly attributable to model quality, because four things differ from the paper's setup:

  • Model class. Gemma 4 E4B is a ~4B-parameter on-device model; the smallest model the paper tests (GPT-4.1 nano) is a far larger, stronger cloud model. Nothing in Gemma's weight class is benchmarked there.
  • Different data. We score an OBGYN-filtered mamabench subset, not the full HealthBench — different question mix.
  • Different, stricter grader. We use gpt-oss-120b; the paper uses GPT-4.1. Per Phase A, our judge under-detects met criteria by ~4–10 pp, biasing our absolute scores downward.
  • Clipping. The paper clips the mean to [0,1]; we keep it unclipped, so our negative hard would read as 0.00 under the paper's convention.

So: low, and below published models on the comparable consensus scale — consistent with a small on-device model on a hard benchmark under a stricter judge — but indicative only, not a leaderboard-comparable result. The within-study ±RAG comparison remains the clean signal.

Caveats & methodology

  • No confidence intervals. All deltas here are point estimates. Treat oss/hard deltas as noise until CIs are computed; the absolute scores and the completeness/penalty findings are robust (large n, direct counts).
  • Judge calibration. Per the Phase A report, gpt-oss-120b's overall met-rate is 73.2% vs a 77% physician baseline (−3.8 pp; closest of all candidates tested), but it under-detects failures on NOT-MET rows (67.9% vs 77.6% human ceiling, ~10 pp). So absolute weighted_met here is a mild lower bound; the ±RAG Δ cancels the bias (same judge, both arms).
  • Track asymmetry. Rubric uses OpenAI's HealthBench grader prompt verbatim (inline per-criterion explanation); SAQ uses a custom verdict-only prompt with captured CoT. Same judge, same ±RAG robustness; only audit surface differs.

Operational: full sweep 4,678 rows / 38,308 criterion calls in ~2 h on a single H200, $0 API. Client concurrency (ThreadPoolExecutor in rescore_rubric.py) capped well below the GPU ceiling — KV-cache utilization peaked at ~1.4% — so raising MAX_WORKERS could cut wall-clock 2–3× if needed.
